Avery turned another page in the folder.

“Tell me about Reed.”

Jill answered cautiously.

“She seemed like she was having a hard time.”

“That was not my question.”

Jill stopped.

Avery watched her quietly across the table.

“Why did you interact with Reed?”

The room felt smaller suddenly.

Jill chose her words carefully.

“I thought she needed somebody to talk to.”

“Did you?”

“Yes ma’am.”

Avery made a small notation.

“Did you intend to violate silence expectations that night?”

“No ma’am.”

“But you did speak after lights-out preparation had already begun.”

Jill hesitated.

“Yes ma’am.”

“Why?”

Jill shifted slightly in the chair.

“I thought helping her mattered more.”

Avery looked down at the folder again.

“What made you think you were the appropriate person to help Marla Reed?”

The question hit harder than Jill expected.

“I don’t know if I was.”

“But you acted anyway.”

“Yes ma’am.”

“Why?”

Jill searched for the answer.

Because she looked broken.  
Because nobody else moved.  
Because I knew what it felt like to disappear in the room.

But none of those sounded safe.

Finally:  
“She talked to me earlier.”

Avery’s eyes lifted immediately.

“That is not the real answer.”

Silence.

Jill looked down briefly at her hands.

“I think… I didn’t want her to feel alone.”

Avery nodded once.

Better.

Then the counselor turned another page.

“What about Grant?”

Jill straightened slightly.

“What about her, ma’am?”

“Why are you helping her?”

“You asked me to.”

Avery tilted her head faintly.

“Did I?”

The question landed like a trap.

Jill suddenly replayed the hallway conversation in her head.

Avery had not technically instructed her.

Only suggested possibility.

Jill realized that now.

Avery continued calmly.

“Are you certain you are the correct person to help Grant?”

“I think I can help her.”

“That was not my question.”

The white-noise machine hummed softly near the wall.

Avery folded her hands.

“Did you perform well academically?”

“Yes ma’am.”

“Was English your best subject?”

“No ma’am.”

“What was?”

Jill hesitated.

“Logic. Analysis. Argument structure.”

Avery nodded slowly.

“Interesting.”

Then immediately:

“How does that qualify you to teach Grant English?”

Jill opened her mouth, then stopped.

Avery pressed forward without changing tone.

“If Grant fails because your instruction confuses her, what then?”

Jill felt her pulse increase.

“I’m trying to help her.”

“That is not protection from consequences.”

Another note on the page.

“Did you consider the possibility that your involvement could damage her confidence before you inserted yourself into her educational process?”

Jill looked up sharply now.

“You asked me to help her.”

Avery remained perfectly calm.

“No, Voss.”

The correction landed cleanly.

“I observed that Grant was struggling.”

Silence.

“I observed that you possess educational advantages.”

Another pause.

“You chose to involve yourself.”

Jill felt suddenly defensive.

“I thought it was the right thing to do.”

“Why?”

The question came immediately.

No emotional padding.  
No rescue.

Jill sat there breathing carefully.

Finally:  
“Because nobody should feel stupid for trying.”

The room became very quiet.

Avery stopped writing.

For several seconds she simply studied Jill across the table.

Then:

“That answer sounded honest.”

Not praise.

Worse.

Recognition.

Avery leaned back slightly in the chair.

“Do you know what I think your largest adjustment problem is right now?”

“No ma’am.”

“You still believe every decision must be perfect before it is allowed to exist.”

Jill stayed still.

Avery continued calmly.

“So when you helped Reed, you feared violating procedure.”

“Yes ma’am.”

“When you helped Grant, you feared damaging her outcome.”

“Yes ma’am.”

Avery nodded once.

“You are trying to eliminate the possibility of failure before acting.”

The counselor closed the folder partway.

“That is impossible inside human systems.”

Jill looked at her quietly.

Avery’s voice remained level.

“You are not being evaluated on perfection, Voss.”

Then after a pause:

“You are being evaluated on adaptation.”

The sentence settled into the room slowly.

Avery reopened the file.

“Now.”

Another page turned.

“Tell me whether Grant responds better to verbal instruction or written examples.”

And just like that, the pressure shifted again — not disappearing, only changing shape.
