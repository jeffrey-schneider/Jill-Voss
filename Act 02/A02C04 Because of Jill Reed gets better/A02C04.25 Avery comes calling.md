Morning study period had already settled into quiet routine when Counselor Avery entered Pod Two again.

Most of the offenders sat at the tables beneath the television with GED packets, handwriting drills, or state-issued reading assignments spread in front of them. The room carried the soft scratch of pencils against paper and occasional page turns beneath the constant ventilation hum.

Avery stopped briefly at the officer station.

Deputy Keller slid the movement clipboard toward her.

Avery listed several names quietly.

Keller nodded once.

“No conflicts.”

“Good.”

Avery gathered her folders and continued down the corridor toward Interview Room Two without another word.

Thirty seconds later the intercom cracked softly overhead.

“Voss, Jill Margaret. Interview Room Two.”

Jill looked up from the GED workbook she had been reviewing with Grant.

Grant immediately looked nervous.

“It’s probably routine,” Jill said quietly.

Grant frowned.

“Nobody says ‘Interview Room Two’ like it’s routine.”

That was fair.

Jill closed the workbook carefully and stood.

The walk down the corridor felt more familiar now, which somehow unsettled her more than the first time had.

When she reached the room, Avery already sat inside with the usual stack of folders arranged neatly beside her.

Medical notes.  
Housing summaries.  
Counseling observations.  
Educational reports.

The entire institution seemed capable of fitting into manila folders.

Avery motioned toward the chair.

“Sit down, Voss.”

“Yes ma’am.”

Jill sat carefully.

Avery adjusted one sheet in the open file but did not immediately begin writing.

For several seconds she simply observed Jill quietly.

Not staring.

Assessing.

Finally Avery spoke.

“What have you learned since our last visit?”

The question caught Jill off guard immediately.

Not:  
How are you?  
Not:  
Any problems?  
Not:  
Are you adjusting?

Learned.

Jill hesitated.

Avery waited comfortably inside the silence.

Jill had started recognizing that as one of Avery’s techniques. She never rushed answers. She allowed people to hear themselves thinking.

Finally Jill spoke carefully.

“I’ve learned that people here notice more than they say.”

Avery nodded once.

“Good.”

Jill continued slowly.

“I think I also learned that not every correction means catastrophe.”

Avery made a small notation.

“What taught you that?”

Jill thought about:  
the haircut,  
the gown notation,  
Martin,  
Reed,  
Grant,  
the breakfast line.

“The fact that things kept going afterward.”

Another note.

Avery looked up again.

“What else?”

Jill frowned slightly.

The room stayed quiet except for the faint white-noise machine humming near the wall.

“I learned that people start disappearing if nobody talks to them.”

Avery’s expression changed almost imperceptibly.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

“And what made you notice that?”

“Reed.”

Avery folded her hands lightly atop the folder.

“You seem less frightened today.”

Jill answered honestly.

“I still am frightened.”

“Yes,” Avery said calmly. “But now you’re functioning while frightened.”

The distinction landed harder than Jill expected.

Avery leaned back slightly in the chair.

“That’s adaptation.”

Not praise.

Not diagnosis.

Observation.

Avery turned another page in the folder.

“Tell me about Grant.”

And just like that, Jill realized the entire conversation had already been steering there from the beginning.
