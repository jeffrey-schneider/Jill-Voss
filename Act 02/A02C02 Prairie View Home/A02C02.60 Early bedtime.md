The television shut off first.

Mid-sentence.

One moment canned laughter echoed softly across the pod. The next moment the screen went black.

Heads lifted almost together.

Nobody spoke.

The overhead lights dimmed a few seconds later. Not fully dark. The facility never became fully dark. The bright white fluorescents dropped into the softer evening setting used during final lockdown.

Only it was not evening yet.

Orange sunlight still stretched through the narrow reinforced windows along the upper wall. Long bands of Oklahoma sunset cut across the concrete floor and table tops.

The clock above the officer station read 1959.

A buzz sounded through the pod speakers.

“Pod Two. Early bedtime is now in effect. Secure to your assigned bunks. No further dayroom activity tonight.”

The speaker clicked off.

For a second nobody moved.

The room held itself still the way people do when hoping a thing might somehow reverse if they wait long enough.

Then chairs scraped.

Not violently.  
Not dramatically.

Just tired.

Women began standing from the tables one at a time. A domino line of defeated motion. Cards stacked away. Books closed. Plastic cups dumped into the bins near the sink.

One offender looked up toward the windows.

The sky outside still looked like late afternoon.

“Unbelievable,” somebody muttered quietly.

“Shut up,” another voice answered immediately.

Nobody wanted the board changed again.

Jill remained near her bunk while movement passed around her. Black-and-white striped pajamas drifted through the dimmed light like tired ghosts. First monthers adjusted their caps automatically as they moved. Some of them still had damp hair from showers. Others carried state blankets folded beneath one arm.

Across the pod, Cam Jonson sat on the edge of her mattress without removing her shoes.

Nobody looked directly at her.

That was worse.

Deputy Keller appeared briefly behind the glass officer station and checked the pod count sheet without expression. He never acknowledged the frustration in the room. To him this looked like compliance.

Which, technically, it was.

The windows continued glowing orange.

One offender climbed onto her upper bunk and stared upward at the concrete ceiling for several seconds before finally pulling the blanket over her shoulder.

Another rolled toward the wall with her back to the room before the lights finished dimming.

The pod settled slowly instead of naturally.

That was the part Jill noticed.

This was not bedtime. The room did not feel tired enough for sleep yet. The air still carried daytime tension. Bodies still held unused energy. The facility had simply declared the day over before anyone was ready for it to end.

At exactly 2005 hours the final row of overhead lights dimmed another step lower.

Outside, the Oklahoma sky was still blue.
