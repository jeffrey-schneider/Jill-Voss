Lights-out had already been called.

The pod rested beneath low evening lighting and the constant soft hum of ventilation moving through concrete and steel. Most of Pod Two had settled into their bunks by now. Occasional mattress springs shifted softly in the darkness while somebody farther down the row turned pages in a state-issued paperback beneath the dimmed night fixture.

Deputy Martin moved slowly through final walk-through carrying her count sheet and flashlight.

She stopped beside Jill’s bunk.

The flashlight beam dropped briefly toward the mattress corners.

“Rack’s looking strack tonight, Voss.”

Jill looked up from beneath the thin blanket.

“Yes ma’am.”

It was the first thing approaching praise Jill had heard since arriving.

Martin made a small notation on the count sheet.

“You’re adjusting faster now.”

Jill did not know whether that was good news or bad news.

“Yes ma’am.”

Martin remained there another moment longer than normal.

Then her voice lowered slightly.

“You notice Reed today?”

Jill glanced across the pod automatically.

Marla Reed lay motionless on her lower bunk facing the concrete wall. She had barely spoken since the haircut day. During evening meal she had pushed food around her tray more than eaten it.

“Yes ma’am.”

Martin watched the bunk area quietly.

“She missed two medication reminders yesterday because she stopped responding when addressed.”

Jill stayed silent.

“Counseling’s tracking it. Medical’s tracking it. So am I.”

The flashlight beam shifted off the floor.

“Some people hit the wall during first month.”

Jill swallowed once.

Martin finally looked directly at her.

“You seem to understand this place better than most this early.”

Jill almost denied it automatically.

Instead she asked quietly:  
“Is she okay?”

Martin considered the question.

“She’s still here.”

That answer felt institutional enough to be honest.

The deputy checked the count sheet once more.

Then:  
“If an offender starts isolating too hard in here, sometimes another offender talking to them helps more than staff does.”

Jill looked back toward Reed’s bunk again.

“Am I allowed to talk to her?”

Martin’s expression barely changed.

“You’re allowed to use appropriate quiet communication before final silence.”

That was not exactly permission.

It also clearly was.

Martin stepped away toward the next bunk.

“Lights fully dim in seven minutes.”

“Yes ma’am.”

The deputy continued her rounds.

Jill remained still for another thirty seconds listening to the ventilation hum overhead.

Then she slid carefully from her bunk and crossed the pod in soft shower sandals.

Nobody stopped her.

Marla Reed did not move when Jill approached.

The woman’s blanket remained pulled tightly around her shoulders despite the warmth in the pod.

Jill stopped beside the bunk quietly.

“Reed?”

No answer.

Jill lowered herself slowly onto the empty edge of the neighboring bunk across the aisle instead of crowding closer.

For several seconds she simply sat there.

Finally:  
“You should probably eat more tomorrow.”

Marla gave the smallest reaction at all. Not turning. Just awareness.

“I’m not hungry.”

Her voice sounded scraped thin.

Jill nodded once even though Reed could not see it.

“The potatoes were terrible anyway.”

A tiny pause.

Then, unexpectedly:  
“They really were.”

The first trace of personality Jill had heard from her all day.

Jill looked toward the dark ceiling.

“When I got here I thought every mistake meant something catastrophic was about to happen.”

Marla finally turned slightly toward her.

“And now?”

Jill thought about Carney.  
Avery.  
The paperwork.  
The haircuts.  
The white board.

“…now I think they just keep moving.”

Marla watched her quietly.

Jill continued:  
“I don’t think they expect us to be okay immediately.”

Silence settled again.

Not empty this time.

Across the pod Deputy Martin completed final count near the officer station without looking toward them once.

Marla slowly pulled the blanket down from around her face.

“I don’t think I’m good at this place.”

Jill answered honestly.

“I don’t think anybody is at first.”

The overhead lights dimmed one final level lower.

Neither woman moved immediately.

For the first time since arriving at the facility, Jill no longer felt entirely alone inside it.
