Avery waited.

Jill sat perfectly still in the chair, hands locked together tightly enough that the knuckles had begun whitening.

The counselor finally closed the folder halfway.

“Voss, you still haven’t answered my questions.”

Jill stared down at the laminate tabletop.

“Pick one,” Avery said calmly. “We’ll explore it.”

Silence.

“If you don’t choose,” Avery continued, “I will.”

The white-noise machine hummed softly near the wall.

Then:

“Let’s discuss why Stephanie Hammond didn’t bail you out last year when you needed her.”

Jill’s head lifted immediately.

“That’s not fair.”

Avery’s expression did not change.

“No?”

Jill looked away.

Avery continued in the same even professional tone.

“You needed help.”

“Yes.”

“She had resources.”

Jill stayed silent.

“She had history with you.”

Another pause.

“She chose not to intervene.”

Jill’s throat tightened visibly now.

Avery leaned slightly forward.

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

“That answer remains statistically unlikely.”

The pressure in the room kept building not through volume, but through refusal to let Jill hide inside vague language.

Avery continued:

“What did you do that convinced Stephanie Hammond leaving you alone was the correct decision?”

Jill’s breathing had become visibly uneven now.

“I hurt her.”

“How?”

Jill looked trapped between answering and refusing.

Avery waited patiently.

No rescue.

No softening.

Finally Jill spoke quietly.

“I kept choosing people who needed saving instead of the person who loved me.”

Avery’s eyes sharpened slightly.

“Interesting distinction.”

Jill pressed forward now almost despite herself.

“Stephanie never needed me to rescue her.”

“No?”

“No.”

“What did she need?”

The question landed brutally hard because it sounded sincere.

Jill closed her eyes briefly.

“She needed me to stay.”

Silence.

Avery did not write that down immediately.

She let it exist first.

Then:  
“And you didn’t.”

Jill shook her head once.

“No.”

Avery leaned back slightly again.

“Why not?”

“I thought…”

Jill stopped.

Avery waited.

“I thought eventually she’d realize I wasn’t worth the damage.”

The room became very quiet after that.

Avery finally made another notation.

Then:

“So you abandoned her emotionally before she could abandon you physically.”

Jill looked up sharply.

“That’s not—”

“Incorrect?”

Avery held her gaze calmly.

“Or uncomfortable?”

Jill had no answer ready this time.

Avery continued:

“You know what interests me most here, Voss?”

Jill said nothing.

“You describe yourself as though you were powerless in these relationships.”

Avery folded her hands again.

“But every story you tell involves choices.”

Another pause.

“You chose Lenore.”

“You chose avoidance.”

“You chose distance.”

“You chose shame.”

The counselor’s voice never rose.

“And now you’re sitting in this room framing Stephanie Hammond as someone who ‘abandoned’ you after you spent years teaching her you would eventually disappear anyway.”

Jill felt tears threaten suddenly and hated herself for it.

Avery noticed immediately.

“Good,” she said quietly.

The word stunned Jill.

“Why is that good?”

“Because now we’re finally near something honest.”

Avery’s tone softened only slightly.

“Voss, I am not trying to humiliate you.”

The counselor tapped the closed folder lightly.

“I am trying to determine whether you intend to keep building your identity around guilt.”

Silence.

“Because guilt feels moral,” Avery continued calmly. “But most of the time it’s just avoidance wearing formal clothing.”

That hit hard enough Jill physically recoiled slightly.

Avery saw that too.

“You’re intelligent enough to survive this institution mechanically,” she said. “What I don’t yet know is whether you’re emotionally brave enough to survive your own life afterward.”

The room stayed silent after that.

Not empty.

Heavy.

And for the first time since arriving at PVH, Jill realized Counselor Avery might actually be trying to return her to the outside world functional instead of merely compliant.
