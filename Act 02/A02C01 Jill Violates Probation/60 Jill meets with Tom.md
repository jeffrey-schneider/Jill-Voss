Tom set the folder down carefully before speaking.

Jill already knew from the weight in the room that nothing inside it was good.

“The State is willing to proceed on revocation,” he said.

She nodded once.

Not trusting herself to speak yet.

Tom leaned back slightly in the chair.

“They are not interested in arguing about whether the violations occurred.”

That landed exactly where he intended it to.

Jill looked down at her hands.

“The driving hurt you,” he continued. “The curfew violation hurt you. The bottle hurt you most of all because it creates appearance problems the Court cannot ignore.”

“I didn’t drink it.”

“I know.”

Tom said it immediately.

No hesitation.

No sarcasm.

“I know you didn’t.”

Jill swallowed hard.

“But appearance matters in probation court,” he said quietly. “Especially this early.”

Silence stretched between them.

Then Tom folded his hands together.

“Schneider is prepared to revoke.”

That one hurt.

Not because she was surprised.

Because hearing it aloud made it real.

“How much?”

“Forty-five to serve. Forty-five held back.”

Jill closed her eyes briefly.

Tillman.

Green uniforms.

Count lights.

Concrete.

Noise.

The smell of intake.

Tom watched her carefully.

Then he delivered the harder part.

“You’re not going back to Mustang afterward.”

Her eyes opened immediately.

“What?”

“The original structure is dead, Jill.”

She stared at him.

“No.”

“Yes.”

His tone remained calm.  
Steady.

“The Court no longer believes the previous arrangement was sufficient.”

“That was my home.”

Tom nodded once.

“I know.”

“That was Stephanie.”

“I know.”

Jill looked away sharply then, jaw tightening.

For several seconds neither of them spoke.

Finally:

“What happens after the forty-five?”

Tom slid a second document across the table.

“Two options.”

She looked down.

Option One:  
Two years intensive supervised probation.

The conditions filled nearly the entire page.

GPS.  
Curfew.  
Vehicle prohibition.  
Reporting.  
Search compliance.  
Therapy.  
Monitoring.

Her stomach tightened.

“That’s prison outside prison,” she whispered.

Tom did not disagree.

“No.”

Her eyes shifted downward.

The second page.

Prairie View House.

Six months.

Residential.

Structured rehabilitation and certification program.

Successful completion may satisfy the Court in lieu of extended intensive supervision.

Jill read the line twice.

Then a third time.

“What’s the catch?”

Tom exhaled softly through his nose.

“That’s the catch.”

She looked up.

“I don’t understand.”

“You don’t survive Prairie View by pretending.”

The room became very quiet.

Tom’s voice lowered slightly.

“Jill… Schneider believes you still compartmentalize under pressure.”

The words hit cleanly because they were true.

“He believes you’re capable of becoming something stronger than this.”

“That sounds optimistic.”

“It isn’t.”

Tom leaned forward slightly now.

“This is the hardest option on the table.”

She frowned faintly.

“It’s six months.”

“No,” Tom said softly. “It’s exposure.”

Jill said nothing.

“You will be observed constantly. Evaluated constantly. Pressed constantly. And at the end, people much more perceptive than either of us are going to decide whether they believe you can safely live free.”

Fear moved visibly across her face then.

Real fear.

Tom watched it happen.

Then finally:

“You need to understand something very clearly.”

Jill looked at him.

“If you choose Prairie View House, you are choosing to let them see you completely.”

Silence.

Long silence.

Then very quietly:

“And if I don’t?”

Tom looked at her for a very long moment before answering.

“Then you spend the next two years proving to the Court that the old Jill is still in control.”

Jill looked back down at the papers.

At the word:  
certification.

At the six months.

At the possibility that everything she thought her life was supposed to be had already ended.

And somewhere underneath the fear…

something else appeared.

Not hope.

Something harder.

The possibility that Schneider might still believe she was salvageable.
